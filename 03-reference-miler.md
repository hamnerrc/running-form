# Reference: running form at mile pace (Athlete B)

The same idea as the sprint analysis, with a different goal. For a miler, form matters in two ways:
1. **Economy:** how much energy each meter costs at race pace.
2. **Speed:** the mechanics of the kick, and of holding form when tired.

These can pull in opposite directions (see "speed vs economy" below), so we measure both.

## What the research says

**Technique matters, but not everything is fixable.**
- In 97 runners, technique explained about **39% of the differences in economy** and **31% of the differences in performance**. [Loughborough / Folland 2017](https://lboro.ac.uk/news-events/news/2017/april/best-technique-running-marathon-research)
- **Variables most linked to economy:** pelvis bounce during ground contact (scaled to height), minimum knee angle during contact, and minimum forward pelvis speed (i.e. braking at each landing). **Variables most linked to performance:** braking, **shin angle at touchdown**, **duty factor** (share of time on the ground), and trunk forward lean. [PubMed 28263283](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28263283/)
- Direction of effects: less vertical bounce, a shin closer to vertical at touchdown, a lower duty factor, and shorter ground contacts all went with better results. [Runner's World summary](https://runnersworldonline.com.au/?p=30903)

**Cadence, stiffness, foot strike.**
- A 2024 meta-analysis (51 studies) linked **higher cadence and higher leg/vertical stiffness** to better economy. **Foot strike type (heel vs mid/forefoot) made no significant difference**, and **more knee bend in swing may actually be worse**. Effects were mostly small to moderate. [Running Physio on Van Hooren et al.](https://www.running-physio.com/efficiency/)
- Trained runners **self-optimize stride length**. Economy is best near the preferred stride length, so big forced changes are not recommended. [Moore 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4887549)
- The parts of ground contact linked most to economy: **less leg extension at toe-off**, alignment of the ground force with the leg, and low muscle activation. [Moore 2016](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s40279-016-0474-4)

**Elite 1500 m specifics.**
- Elite 1500 m runners (2005 World Champs) kept a **straighter knee in mid-stance** and extended it more slowly than national-level runners. The authors read this as better use of elastic energy with less concentric work. [Elite vs national 1500 m](https://lida.sport-iat.de/ta/Record/4015913) (Folland's large study linked more knee bend in stance with better economy, so this one is genuinely mixed.)
- **Speed vs economy:** in the 2017 World Champs final, the best finishers had **longer contacts, more overstriding, and lower stiffness**, plus higher horizontal forces. The authors suggest elite milers may favor a **speed-oriented technique over pure economy**. Runners went faster by raising cadence, not step length. [Hanley et al. 2023](https://eprints.glos.ac.uk/12365/)
- Over a 1500 m time trial, collegiate runners' turnover and step length dropped after 300 m, and contact times were longest at 1100 m. Fatigue shows up in form. [ISBS 2005](https://ojs.ub.uni-konstanz.de/cpa/article/view/1040/953)

## Key positions: what professional middle-distance analysis looks at

Professional analysis of distance runners uses the same key positions as sprinting (touchdown, full support,
toe-off, swing), with a different priority: economy at race pace, and sprint mechanics for the kick.

**World 1500 m final (Hanley et al. 2023, 2017 World Championships men's final, laps at 7.0–7.5 m/s; 3-D).**

**Elite vs non-elite distance runners (what separates them).**
- **Shin closer to vertical at touchdown** (high-performance runners 4.4° closer to vertical, Preece 2019) and a
  **more upright trunk** that doesn't lean further forward as speed rises (Preece; Folland 2017: a more upright trunk
  went with better performance). [Preece 2019](https://salford-repository.worktribe.com/output/1375358)
- High-performance runners have **longer flight times** (+11%) with a higher vertical take-off speed, i.e. they keep
  step length with a longer flight rather than by reaching with the foot (Preece 2019). This sits against Folland's
  "less vertical oscillation is more economical", so vertical oscillation on its own is a weak target.
- **Swing leg:** high-performance runners fold the knee more in swing (tighter heel recovery), with no difference in
  knee angle at touchdown ([running styles synthesis, Sports Biomech 2021](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/14763141.2021.1873411)).
- **Sprinters vs distance runners at the same speed** (Bushnell & Hunter 2007, 5.81 m/s and max speed): they differ
  in minimum hip angle, **knee extension at toe-off** (distance runners push to a straighter knee), and **recovery
  knee position at touchdown** (distance runners' swing knee further back). The distance runners kept that pattern
  when sprinting, and the authors suggest technique work for the end of races.
  [Sports Biomech 6(3)](https://sponet.de/sponet/Record/4013216)

**What this means for the comparison.** There is no published swing-leg-at-touchdown value for elite milers at mile
pace, so the swing-leg numbers at mile pace are compared with his own kick and with the general distance-runner
pattern above, not graded. In the **kick** (7.6 m/s) the sprint references apply (Clark 2025's team-sport group at
8.2 m/s is the closest published group: swing thigh at touchdown −10 ± 8°, swing knee 66 ± 6°, knee lift 65 ± 5°,
thigh angular velocity 409 ± 22 °/s).

## How recommendations will be framed
- **Change little.** Trained runners self-optimize, so only clear outliers (heavy braking, big bounce, a large asymmetry) get a cue. 1–2 cues at most.
- **Economy at mile pace, speed at the kick.** We won't push the kick toward "economical" if the research says elites trade economy for speed there.
- **Foot strike gets no cue** unless it creates braking.
